Fort Myers Beach Hurricane Ian Recovery Dashboard

Three and a half years after Hurricane Ian made Category 4 landfall just north of the island, Fort Myers Beach's lodging capacity has rebuilt to 66% of pre-Ian inventory, while bed-tax revenue has reached 48% of pre-Ian levels. The 18-point gap between capacity and revenue is part of the story: rooms are coming back, but per-room revenue hasn't fully returned. Another 877 lodging units are confirmed in development across 9 projects (plus 15 residential units in 2 housing projects, tracked separately). This dashboard tracks the measurable signals, with baselines sourced from the FMB Chamber and Lee County.

Tourism & Lodging Capacity

Lodging is FMB's economic engine. Pre-Ian, the town was Lee County's #1 bed-tax generator at $8.2M/year — 12.4% of the county total. Tourism capacity has rebuilt unevenly: hotels lag, vacation rentals lead.

Capacity vs Revenue

Two strong indicators tell different parts of the same story. Lodging capacity has rebuilt to 66% of pre-Ian inventory; bed-tax revenue has reached only 48%. The 18-point gap is the recovery story behind the recovery story.

Recovery to date — capacity vs revenue
Both measured against pre-Ian baselines (FY22 YTD for bed tax; pre-Ian room inventory per FMB Chamber).

What the 18-point gap means: capacity is rebuilding faster than per-room revenue. Each rebuilt unit is generating less revenue than its pre-Ian counterpart — a function of lower occupancy, lower nightly rates, or both. As visitor confidence and seasonal bookings normalize, the gap should narrow. If it doesn't, the story is no longer "recovery isn't done" but "FMB has fewer high-revenue rooms than it used to." Worth watching quarter over quarter.

Lodging Mix Shift

FMB's recovery is structurally different from Sanibel's. Hotels were heavily destroyed and rebuild slowly; vacation rentals returned faster. The result is a town economically more dependent on short-term rentals than ever before.

About the Data

Methodology and exclusions, paralleling the Sanibel Solutions recovery framework.

Methodology Rules

  1. Compare each metric to its pre-Ian baseline where available.
  2. Cap recovery percentage at 100% (exceeding baseline counts as fully recovered).
  3. When a baseline is not yet verified, mark "baseline pending" rather than fabricate a number.
  4. Exclude inverse indicators (e.g., real estate listing volume — high inventory signals distress, not recovery).
  5. Surface leading indicators (delinquent VR licenses, café closure concentration) as Watchlist, not in the headline.

What This Q1 2026 Snapshot Includes

  • Bed tax recovery (48%): the revenue indicator — full 4-year YTD series (FY22–FY25 through August) from Lee County Clerk of Court.
  • Lodging capacity recovery (66%): hotel rooms (76%) and vacation rental rooms (63%) — direct from FMB Chamber of Commerce, March 2026.
  • Capacity vs Revenue analysis: the 18-point gap between capacity and revenue is featured as its own analytical section.
  • Sister-city comparison: bed-tax recovery side-by-side for FMB / Sanibel / Fort Myers / Estero.
  • Pipeline: 877 lodging units confirmed across 9 projects (665 hotel + 212 VR); 2 projects pending plans; 15 residential units in a separate housing pipeline. Verified May 23, 2026.
  • Permanent losses: 816 rooms lost forever (590 hotel + 226 VR) — sets the ceiling on recovery.
  • Economic activity: restaurants, retail, marinas, total businesses.
  • Watchlist signals: delinquent VR licenses, café closures, currently-closed businesses, population context.

Why Bed Tax is the Lead Metric

Among all the indicators on this dashboard, bed tax is the single most credible signal of tourism recovery. It captures total revenue (rooms × occupancy × rate × spend), is collected and audited monthly by the Lee County Clerk of Court, and is reported on the same basis across municipalities — enabling apples-to-apples comparison between FMB, Sanibel, and the rest of Lee County. Inventory counts (hotel rooms, VR licenses) measure capacity; bed tax measures actual revenue.

What's Excluded (and Why)

  • Real estate sales / listings: active market volume can indicate distress as easily as health (Sanibel's exclusion).
  • Permits issued / closed: measures reconstruction activity, not return to normalcy.
  • Population without context: flat year-over-year ACS numbers can hide significant displacement and re-arrival; shown as context not as a recovery metric.
